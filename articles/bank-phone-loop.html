The Bank & Phone Loop in Japan

In Japan, opening a bank account and getting a mobile phone number can feel like a loop. A bank may ask for a phone number, while a phone company may ask for a bank account or Japanese payment method.

Quick answer

The loop happens because banks, phone companies, and payment services all want a way to confirm your identity, contact you, and collect payment. Before applying, check the chosen bank or phone provider’s requirements for identity documents, current address, contact method, and payment. Requirements vary by service and application method.

Why this happens

For many services in Japan, companies need to know where you live, how to contact you, and how you will pay. If one of those pieces is missing, the application may become harder.

  • Banks may want a Japanese phone number so they can contact you or verify your application.
  • Mobile phone companies may want a bank account, credit card, or payment method.
  • Online services may ask for SMS verification, which needs a number that can receive SMS and is accepted by that service.
  • Some applications may be harder if your address is not registered yet.

A practical order that may help

This is a starting point, not a required order for everyone. Check what you already have and what each application needs.

1. Register your address
Go to your city or ward office after moving in and register your address on your Residence Card.
2. Prepare a contact method
Ask which contact number the service accepts. Before buying a temporary SIM or eSIM, check whether it supports the calls or SMS you need; data-only access is not enough for SMS verification.
3. Check a bank’s application requirements
Confirm accepted ID, current address, contact number, and any residence-period or employment requirements for your application method. Approval is not guaranteed.
4. Set up phone payment
Check whether your chosen phone plan accepts your bank account or card, and whose name the payment method must be in.

Common misunderstandings

“I cannot do anything without a bank account.”

Not always. Some services may accept a credit card, prepaid option, or another payment method.

“All banks have the same rules.”

No. Requirements can differ by bank, branch, visa status, length of stay, employer, and available documents.

“A foreign phone number should be enough.”

Sometimes it is not enough. Many Japanese services expect a Japanese phone number for contact or SMS verification.

“If one company says no, everything is impossible.”

No. A different bank, phone plan, or order of applications may work better.

What to prepare before applying

Check which of these your chosen service requires; this is not a universal document list.

  • Residence Card
  • Address already registered at the city or ward office
  • Passport
  • Student ID, employee ID, or work/school document if you have one
  • A contact method such as a phone number or email address
  • A payment method, if available

Requirements differ, so check the company’s official instructions before going.

🐱 Cat tip: The loop feels personal, but it is usually just paperwork logic. Change the order, prepare documents, and try again.

Summary

The bank and phone loop is common for new residents in Japan. It happens because each service wants identity, address, contact, and payment information. Check address registration and each provider’s current requirements, then choose an order that works with the documents, contact method, and payment option you have.

Check the official requirements

These are examples, not recommendations or rules for every provider. Check the specific product and whether you will apply online or in person.

  • Japan Post Bank: accounts for foreign residents (Japanese) explains residence documents, remaining stay, and additional school or work documents.
  • SMBC Trust Bank PRESTIA: account opening (Japanese) separates online and branch requirements. Its online application conditions include a domestic mobile number able to receive SMS.
  • au: identification and payment documents at shops (Japanese) lists documents, address checks, and payment options.
  • IIJmio: SIM options for visitors and residents distinguishes data-only, SMS, and voice plans. Check the chosen plan’s ID and payment requirements.

SIM types: Data-only service provides internet access, not ordinary voice calls or SMS. SMS support does not by itself mean voice calls are included. A SIM or eSIM label alone does not tell you which functions are available.

If you hold a 特定在留カード: This card combines Residence Card and My Number Card functions under the system introduced on 14 June 2026 (government explanation, Japanese PDF). Japan Post Bank’s guide includes it, and au’s shop guide lists it. IIJmio’s ID guide (Japanese) also lists it but notes differences at retail counters. Do not assume every bank, provider, or application channel accepts it in the same way; check before applying.
